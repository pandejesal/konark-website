Interlock philosophy: why safety interlocks exist, and why they get bypassed

By Nilesh Pande, Founder and Lead Engineer, Konark Process Instruments

Why this matters

Every burner management system ships with interlocks - conditions that shut the system down when something is wrong. And in almost every plant that has been running for a few years, some of those interlocks have been bypassed. The bypass is not always stupidity. It is usually the result of a real conflict: the interlock trips too often, production suffers, and someone makes a judgment call. Understanding why interlocks get bypassed is the first step to designing systems that do not need to be bypassed - and to auditing the ones that are.

What an interlock actually is

An interlock is a condition that prevents or stops a dangerous action. In a furnace system, the classic set is:

  1. Purge-before-light-off. Fuel cannot be admitted until a timed purge has cleared the combustion chamber with proven airflow.

2. Flame supervision. If the flame signal is lost, the fuel valves close - immediately, not after discussion. 3. Pressure and flow proof. Low combustion air, low gas pressure, failed draft: each one closes the fuel supply. 4. High-temperature and limit devices. Process limits and mechanical limits protect the equipment as well as the people. 5. Start interlock chains. The system can only start from the correct state, in the correct order.

Why interlocks get bypassed - the four real reasons

  1. Nuisance trips. The interlock fires on a sensor fault, a vibration, or a marginal reading that is not a real hazard. After the third false trip at 2 AM, an operator makes the decision nobody documented.

2. The "it never happens" calculation. The plant has run ten years without the event the interlock protects against. Familiarity makes the risk feel abstract. 3. Pressure from production. A shutdown costs money now. The bypass is "temporary" - but temporary bypasses are the most permanent objects in industrial history. 4. Lack of consequence knowledge. Nobody has ever shown the operator what the interlock is protecting against. An interlock with an unknown purpose is an interlock with no authority.

Why bypasses are dangerous even when nothing happens

A bypassed interlock does not fail loudly. It fails silently - the protection is simply absent until the day the event actually occurs. And on that day, the sequence of events includes an operator who believed the system was protecting them. This is why bypasses are a management problem, not just an engineering one: they must be visible, documented, time-limited and reviewed - or they must be physically impossible.

What good systems do about this

  1. Design out nuisance trips. The right sensor, correctly installed, with proper filtering and alarm deadbands, trips on real faults and not on noise. Most nuisance-trip problems are instrument problems, not logic problems.

2. Make bypasses visible and logged. A bypassed input that shows as a live alarm, with the operator name and time logged, is a decision - not a secret. 3. Time-limit bypasses. A bypass that expires automatically forces the discussion that a permanent bypass never gets. 4. Audit the panel. The walkthrough in our furnace safety audit article is exactly how a plant discovers the jumper that nobody remembers installing. 5. Explain the interlock to the operator. An operator who knows what the flame-loss interlock protects against treats it differently from an operator who sees it as a nuisance.

What an owner should ask

  1. Show me every bypass in the system - documented, logged and justified, or removed?

2. What is the false-trip rate, and what is being done about the sensors, not just the symptoms? 3. Who decides that a bypass is acceptable, and what is the review cycle? 4. Is the purge and flame-loss protection physically bypassable at all?

The Konark position (1 paragraph, first-person, no invented data)

"Twenty-plus years of burner management has taught me that interlocks are not engineering decoration - they are the last line of defence, and they only survive if they trip for real reasons. We build systems that trip on faults, not noise, and we audit panels where bypasses have become habits. The answer to a nuisance trip is a better instrument and a better logic design - never a jumper." - Nilesh Pande, Konark Process Instruments
