DG-set automation: what your factory does in the ten seconds of a power failure

By Nilesh Pande, Founder and Lead Engineer, Konark Process Instruments

Why this matters

When grid power fails, a factory has two problems. The first is the obvious one: the machines stop. The second is quieter and more expensive: the sequence in which they come back determines whether the factory is back in ten minutes or four hours. Manual DG starting is not slow because people are slow - it is slow because a person has to think through a sequence that should be automatic: start the genset, check the voltage and frequency, change over the breaker, then re-energise loads in order.

The failure sequence nobody plans for

  1. The blackout. Grid fails. Every motor that was running becomes a restart candidate.

2. The confusion. In a plant without automation, operators race to the DG room, start the set, and change over by hand - while other operators are restarting machines blindly. 3. The inrush problem. Everything that was on the bus wants to start at once. The DG set, sized for running load, gets slammed with starting current and either trips on overload or drags the frequency down until everything trips on under-frequency. 4. The second blackout. Now the plant has two failures to explain: the grid failure and the DG failure that should never have happened.

What DG-set automation (AMF) actually does

An automatic mains failure (AMF) panel replaces the manual sequence with a proven one:

  1. Detection. The panel sees the grid fail - voltage and frequency on all phases, not a single-phase guess.

2. Sequential start. The DG set starts, and the panel waits for the set to reach stable voltage and frequency before anything else moves. 3. Changeover. The changeover happens only when the set is proven - never before. 4. Load-shedding and re-energising. The largest motors come back in a planned order, either by priority or with staggered delays, so the set never sees a full-bus inrush. 5. Return and cooldown. When the grid returns, the changeover is made safely, the DG runs its cooldown period, and it shuts down by itself - or the plant chooses a test run on schedule.

The costs that hide in a manual system

  • Lost production minutes are counted in the gap between the failure and a proven, stable changeover - automation cuts this from minutes to seconds.
  • DG damage from bad changeovers - closing a breaker on an unproven set or against a live bus is how generators get destroyed.
  • Fuel and wear from unnecessary running - a set left running after the grid returns burns diesel for no reason until someone notices.
  • Process damage from uncontrolled restart - for furnaces and continuous processes, uncontrolled re-energising can corrupt a batch or shock a refractory lining.

What an owner should ask

  1. What is the total changeover time from grid failure to proven load?

2. How is load sequencing handled - staggered delays, priority shedding, or both? 3. Is the panel tested with a real load rejection and a simulated blackout before dispatch? 4. What happens when the grid returns - auto-return, or manual with cooldown? 5. Can the DG room run unattended, and what alarms reach a phone?

The Konark position (1 paragraph, first-person, no invented data)

"A power failure is not the event that costs a plant money - the restart is. We build DG-set automation and AMF panels that take the ten seconds of chaos out of a blackout: the set starts, proves itself, changes over and brings the loads back in order. The sequence is tested in the workshop against a simulated failure, not discovered during a real one." - Nilesh Pande, Konark Process Instruments
