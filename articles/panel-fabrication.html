Control panel fabrication: the twelve things a buyer should inspect before dispatch

By Nilesh Pande, Founder and Lead Engineer, Konark Process Instruments

Why this matters

A control panel is where every engineering decision a plant makes - the PLC, the drives, the interlocks, the drawings - gets converted into metal, wire and terminals. Panels look similar from the outside. They are not similar inside. A panel that is poorly built will cost a plant more in downtime, fault-finding and safety exposure than any difference in the purchase price. The problem is that most of the quality is invisible after the doors close. So the inspection has to happen before dispatch - and here is what to look at.

The twelve checks

  1. The rating plate and the environment. Is the panel rated for where it will actually live? IP54 for a clean electrical room is not the same as a panel going next to a furnace where dust and heat are the norm. Check the specified IP against the real environment, not the brochure.

2. Busbar sizing. Ask for the calculation, not the verbal assurance. Busbars are sized for the maximum through-fault and the continuous load - and the distance between supports matters as much as the copper cross-section.

3. Terminal quality. Every field wire ends in a terminal. Crimped, not soldered; ferruled for multi-strand wire; numbered on both the wire and the terminal rail. If the first thing you do in a fault is trace a wire, numbered terminals are what make that possible in minutes instead of hours.

4. Wire routing. Power and signal wiring should not share the same duct over long runs - induction from motor cables corrupts thermocouple and analogue signals. Look for separation between power, control and instrument wiring.

5. Earthing discipline. A single point for the earth bar, bonded door hinges, and a dedicated earth for the PLC/SCADA rail. A panel without earthing discipline is a panel that will produce phantom trips during lightning season.

6. Component access. Can a maintenance person reach every fuse, relay and terminal without removing three other components? If the builder had to squeeze everything in, maintenance will pay for it forever.

7. Labelled everything. Terminal rails, fuses, relays, breakers, field connections - labels on the inside that match the drawings. A panel that relies on memory is a black box with a door.

8. The drawing set. As-built schematics, not pre-build schematics. If the drawings do not match the panel after the last wiring change, the panel is already obsolete on day one.

9. Cable entry. Are glands fitted and sealed? Dust and moisture enter through cable entries, and it is the cheapest part of the build to get wrong.

10. The factory test record. Ask to see the test protocol: continuity, insulation resistance, I/O checks, sequence simulation. A panel that was only "wired and shipped" has never been proven.

11. Spares and consumables. One of each fuse, relay and spare terminals, in a labelled box in the panel. Cheap insurance that saves a day of waiting later.

12. The documentation folder. Manuals for every component inside, in one place. If the builder cannot produce the folder, the component manuals will be scattered across three engineers' desks by the end of the first year.

Why these checks matter more than the brand of the PLC

The PLC, the drives and the software are the visible choices - and they matter. But a panel with an excellent PLC and sloppy wiring fails the same as a panel with a cheap PLC and sloppy wiring. The difference between a good panel and a bad one shows up in the first three faults, not in the first three months.

What an owner should ask for

  1. A factory test protocol and the signed record for this specific panel

2. The as-built drawing set, marked up after every change 3. A walkthrough of the earthing and wire-separation philosophy 4. The documentation folder with every component manual 5. A visit to the workshop while the panel is still open - before dispatch, not after arrival

The Konark position (1 paragraph, first-person, no invented data)

"We build panels the way we would want to maintain them: separated wiring, numbered terminals, labelled components, earthing discipline and a test record that proves the logic was run before dispatch. A panel is not a box of parts - it is the maintenance team's working surface for the next fifteen years, and we build it like that." - Nilesh Pande, Konark Process Instruments
