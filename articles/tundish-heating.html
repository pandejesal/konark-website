Tundish heating: why the temperature of an empty vessel decides steel quality

By Nilesh Pande, Founder and Lead Engineer, Konark Process Instruments

Why this matters

In continuous casting, the tundish is the vessel that sits between the ladle and the mould. Everyone watches the steel temperature in the ladle and the mould, but the tundish itself is the weak link: if it is cold, or unevenly heated, the first steel that enters it cools, and that cooler steel flows straight into the first cast. The result is breakouts, clogging and quality rejects that have nothing to do with the ladle metallurgy and everything to do with a vessel nobody controlled.

What happens when a tundish is not heated properly

  1. Skulling. Steel that touches a cold lining freezes on contact. A skulled tundish has less volume, worse flow and a longer clean-out - and every skulled tundish shortens the lining life.

2. First-cast defects. The first few metres of strand carry the thermal shock of a cold vessel. Surface defects and inclusion pickup concentrate in exactly the product a customer is watching. 3. Refractory damage. Thermal cycling cracks linings. A tundish that is heated unevenly develops hot and cold spots, and the cracks start where the temperature changes fastest. 4. Lining dry-out failures. New or repaired linings contain moisture. Heating them too fast drives the moisture out as steam that damages the refractory; heating them too slowly wastes hours of sequence time.

What automatic tundish heating should do

The classic problem is manual control: an operator opens a burner, watches a thermocouple, and adjusts. The sequence runs overnight or between heats, when nobody is watching closely - which is exactly when the temperature drifts. An automatic system (the type of system often built on Dumac burner technology) does four things a person cannot do reliably:

  1. Ramp the temperature. A controlled heating curve - slow at first to dry the lining, then a steady climb - instead of a blast that cracks the refractory.

2. Hold the temperature. Once at operating temperature, the system holds it with a modulating burner instead of letting it sag between heats. 3. Prove the flame. Every heating phase runs under supervised flame detection, so a burner that dies does not quietly cool the vessel. 4. Log the curve. The recorded temperature profile is evidence for the quality team and the refractory supplier - what the lining actually saw, not what someone remembers setting.

What an owner should ask before buying a tundish heating system

  1. Can you show me the heating curve for a cold, damp lining - ramp rates, hold points and total time?

2. What is the fuel consumption per heat at idle-hold? That number runs 24 hours a day. 3. What happens on a flame failure - does the system purge and re-light itself, or does it sit cold? 4. How many tundishes does the system handle, and how fast does it switch between them? 5. Who supports the automation locally when the caster is running at 3 AM?

The Konark position (1 paragraph, first-person, no invented data)
